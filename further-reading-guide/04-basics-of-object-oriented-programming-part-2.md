## Further Reading — Basics of Object-Oriented Programming Part 2

> **Content note:** Despite the title "Basics of Object-Oriented Programming Part 2," this chapter covers debugging as causal diagnosis — forming a falsifiable hypothesis before touching code, isolating intermediate state with a breakpoint, and tracing from symptom to root cause. The assessments sidebar lists OOP topics (wrapper classes, strings), but the chapter prose and exercises are entirely about the hypothesize-isolate-test workflow. This guide addresses the actual chapter content.

The chapter teaches you *that* you should form a hypothesis before setting a breakpoint — but it does not explain the underlying Java mechanics that make certain bugs (shared mutable references, missed resets between method calls) structurally possible in the first place, nor does it ground the workflow in the formal debugging literature that defines what a "good" hypothesis actually looks like. Those two gaps are the most consequential ones to close before the lab: without the mechanical understanding, your hypotheses will be vague; without the formal vocabulary, you will not know when to revise a hypothesis versus when to abandon it. Read the Key resource before Exercise 4 or the lab — it directly supports the written hypothesis and root-cause explanation the exercise requires. Choose one Recommended resource based on where your understanding feels shakiest: pick the Zeller if the conceptual loop (what makes a hypothesis falsifiable vs. a restatement of the symptom) felt unclear, or pick the Eck section if reading a reference chain across method boundaries still feels uncertain. You do not need to read both. The Further item is optional, requires roughly two hours, and is written for working practitioners — skip it unless the lab has already given you debugging sessions to reflect on.

### Key

**Effective Java, Third Edition** — Joshua Bloch, 2018 (Addison-Wesley Professional) | ISBN 978-0134685991

I included this because the chapter ends with its most important question — "how do you design a system with fewer hidden bugs?" — and never answers it. Items 17 ("Minimize mutability") and 15 ("Minimize the accessibility of classes and members") are that answer. Item 17 explains the structural source of the sequence-dependent checkout bug: when an object can be modified after creation and its reference can be shared across methods, a field can silently hold a stale or wrong value with no stack trace and no announcement. Item 15 shows the access-control mechanism that limits how widely a field can be reached and therefore corrupted. Read Item 17 first (it is the causal argument), then Item 15 (it is the enforcement mechanism). After reading, return to the checkout bug you diagnosed and identify which principle — if applied to `currentPatron` — would have made the bug structurally impossible rather than merely detectable. This is exactly the thinking Exercise 9 asks you to make explicit before Module 5. Focus on the two items named above; the rest of the book can wait.

> Supports: Exercise 9 (Challenge) — naming a Java language feature or design pattern that makes sequence-dependent bugs structurally impossible and explaining the mechanism by which it does so.

### Recommended

**Why Programs Fail: A Guide to Systematic Debugging** — Andreas Zeller, 2nd ed., 2009 (Morgan Kaufmann) | ISBN 978-0123745156

Zeller formalizes the hypothesize-isolate-test workflow this module teaches informally, and the formalization matters for a specific reason: it gives you a criterion for knowing whether your hypothesis is actually falsifiable or is just a restatement of the symptom. The module draws that distinction in prose; Zeller makes it precise, defining the conditions under which a hypothesis is well-formed and working through the logical structure of the "scientific method applied to debugging" in a way that directly supports Exercises 1 and 2. Read Chapter 2 ("Tracking the Problem") — focus carefully on the first half (the conceptual argument about what separates a hypothesis from an observation), then skim the tool discussion in the second half, which uses a different debugger than NetBeans but whose principles transfer directly. After reading, write one sentence comparing Zeller's "failure" / "defect" distinction to the chapter's symptom / root-cause distinction — whether they are equivalent is a question worth being able to answer. Available through most university library systems; check your institution's database access.

**Introduction to Programming Using Java, 9th Edition** — David J. Eck, 2022 | Freely available at https://math.hws.edu/javanotes/

I included this because the chapter assumes you can trace a reference chain — follow a variable from one method into another and identify where its value changes — but never explains the Java mechanism that makes that tracing necessary in the first place: the difference between passing a primitive value (a copy is passed; the original cannot be affected) and passing an object reference (a copy of the reference is passed; the object it points to can be modified). Without that distinction, a hypothesis about *why* `currentPatron` holds the wrong reference at a given moment is incomplete. Read Section 4.3 ("Parameters, Variables, and Scope") first; if pass-by-value for references is not yet solid, close that gap before the lab. Section 3.3 ("Introduction to Error Handling") is worth skimming afterward for context on what the runtime does and does not report automatically. The resource is freely available online, no login required.

### Further

**Debugging: The 9 Indispensable Rules for Finding Even the Most Elusive Software and Hardware Problems** — David J. Agans, 2002 (AMACOM) | ISBN 978-0814471684

This is practitioner-level literature written for working engineers who already form and test hypotheses routinely — it is not a textbook, and it does not teach the workflow from scratch. To get the payoff Agans offers, you should already be comfortable with the module's full three-step loop: not just knowing what a breakpoint is, but having used one to confirm or refute a hypothesis at least once, ideally after the lab. The prerequisite is that you can describe, from your own debugging session, a specific moment when you almost changed something before forming a hypothesis — because what Agans documents, in Chapters 1 through 5 ("Understand the System" through "Change One Thing at a Time"), are exactly the psychological failure modes that cause experienced engineers to abandon the logic under pressure: the "it should work" trap, the "I only changed one thing" rationalization, the "let's just try this" reflex the module teaches you to resist. The payoff the Recommended tier does not offer is scope and stakes — Zeller formalizes the logic; Agans shows what it costs in production systems when the logic breaks down. If you read this after the lab with a real debugging session to reflect on, Chapters 3 and 5 in particular will map onto moments in your own work. Widely available in print; some institutional library systems carry it electronically.
