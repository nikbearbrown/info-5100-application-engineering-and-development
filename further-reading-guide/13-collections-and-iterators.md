## Further Reading — Collections and Iterators

> **Content note:** Despite the title "Collections and Iterators," this chapter covers JUnit testing, assertion-based requirement specification, boundary-case taxonomy, regression testing, and lambda/stream operations. The Java Collections Framework appears in the stated learning objectives and key-term glossary, but the chapter's teaching focus is making behavioral claims executable through tests. This guide addresses the actual chapter content.

The chapter teaches you to state requirements as executable assertions, but it stops short of three things practitioners depend on: a systematic method for generating the cases a method actually needs, the test-first discipline that forces requirement precision before implementation begins, and the theoretical argument for why a passing test is inherently a claim rather than a proof. The questions the chapter raises — why does the null-input test reveal an assumption the loop test does not? how many tests are enough? what does "correct" mean when tests can never be exhaustive? — are live professional debates, not settled conclusions. These resources close those gaps.

Read the Key resource before Exercise 4, where you will write a five-case test suite for your project's search method and need the full JUnit 5 annotation and assertion reference. Then choose one Recommended resource based on which gap felt largest: the first if you want a structured method for generating boundary cases beyond the five-case taxonomy; the second if you want to see how writing tests before code changes what "stating a requirement" means. The Further resource is for students who want the theoretical foundation underlying the chapter's central claim — it is not required, and most students will get more from it after completing the exercises than before.

### Key

**JUnit 5 User Guide** — The JUnit Team, 2024 | https://junit.org/junit5/docs/current/user-guide/

I included this because the chapter uses `@Test`, `assertEquals`, `assertNull`, and `assertThrows` throughout without documenting the full annotation set or explaining how those methods behave when assertions fail. The JUnit 5 User Guide is the authoritative reference for exactly what Exercises 4 and 6 require. Section 2.3 ("Annotations") covers `@Test`, `@BeforeEach`, and `@DisplayName` with working code examples; Section 2.4 ("Assertions") covers `assertEquals`, `assertTrue`, `assertNull`, `assertThrows`, and the overloads that accept failure messages. Read Sections 2.3 and 2.4 — approximately 20–30 minutes — before attempting Exercise 4. Pay particular attention to the `assertThrows` entry: the null-input test case in Exercise 4 requires deciding whether null should throw an exception or return an empty list, and `assertThrows` is the assertion method for the first branch of that decision. Use the guide as a reference during the exercises rather than reading it end to end; when an assertion behaves differently than expected, the User Guide is the place to check the specification. Available free at the stable URL above.

> Supports: Exercise 4 — writing the five-case test suite for the project's search method, including `assertThrows` for the null-input case and `assertEquals` with size assertions for the multi-result case.

### Recommended

**Pragmatic Unit Testing in Java 8 with JUnit** — Jeff Langr, 2015 | ISBN 978-1-941222-14-2 (Pragmatic Bookshelf; series editors Andy Hunt and Dave Thomas)

I included this because the chapter gives you five named cases — normal, empty, no-match, boundary, invalid input — but does not give you a generative method for discovering which boundary cases a specific method needs before you run it. Langr's CORRECT mnemonic in Chapter 7, "Boundary Conditions," provides that method: Conformance, Ordering, Range, Reference, Existence, Cardinality, Time — a checklist that maps directly onto the five-case taxonomy you already have and applies to any method whose behavior varies at edges. Read Chapter 7 before Exercise 8, where you extend the search method to match on author names as well as titles and need to generate the boundary cases for the new requirement before writing the implementation. The CORRECT mnemonic and the chapter's five-case taxonomy describe the same concept at different levels of resolution; reading both will let you apply boundary thinking to methods you encounter professionally, not just the worked examples here. Available through Pragmatic Bookshelf, O'Reilly Learning, and most university libraries.

**Test-Driven Development: By Example** — Kent Beck, 2002 | ISBN 978-0-321-14653-3 (Addison-Wesley; available via institutional library)

I included this because the chapter's AI-boundary rule — write the assertion before asking AI to suggest cases — is the test-first discipline Beck formalized, and understanding why it matters makes the rule legible rather than arbitrary. Part I ("The Money Example," Chapters 1–17) demonstrates the complete red-green-refactor cycle on a domain example worked from scratch, showing how writing the test first forces you to state the requirement precisely before any implementation inference is possible. Read Chapters 1 through 3 — approximately 40 pages — after completing Exercises 4 and 5. The TDD cycle will map directly onto what you experienced when a test initially failed and revealed an assumption that had been invisible in the implementation: that moment is the core of what Beck is formalizing. Reading the chapter's AI boundary section alongside Chapters 1–3 makes clear that the module's pedagogy is not an AI policy; it is a restatement of a 20-year-old professional discipline applied to a new tool. The 2002 edition remains the standard reference and is widely available in print and through O'Reilly Learning.

### Further

**"A Theory of Testing"** — Glenford Myers, Corey Sandler, and Tom Badgett, in *The Art of Software Testing, Third Edition*, Chapter 2 (Wiley, 2011) | ISBN 978-1-118-03196-4 (available via Wiley Online Library with institutional access or university library in print)

This is specialist literature written for practitioners and software engineers with prior testing experience, not a student text — plan for 45–60 minutes and expect some formal definitions to require re-reading. Before this chapter rewards you, you need to have completed Exercise 4 and experienced firsthand the difference between a test passing and the method being correct: that experience is the prerequisite, because the chapter gives you the theoretical argument for why that gap is not a practical limitation but a structural property of testing itself. Myers et al. define testing as the process of executing a program with the intent of finding errors — not confirming correctness — and derive the limits of coverage from the combinatorial impossibility of exhaustive input enumeration. That argument is exactly the theoretical foundation for the chapter's central claim that a passing test is a claim about a specific input-output pair, not a proof about all inputs. What the Recommended resources give you is technique and workflow; what Chapter 2 of Myers et al. gives you is the principled account of why the technique works the way it does and why no amount of technique eliminates the gap. This resource pays off most clearly when you attempt Exercise 9, where you are asked to reason about the limits of the unit test suite you have built and describe a failure it cannot catch — Myers et al. give you the vocabulary and the argument to do that reasoning rigorously rather than by intuition.
